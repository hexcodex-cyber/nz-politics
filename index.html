General election

Saturday 7 November 2026

A plain-language catch-up on New Zealand's parties and their leading figures. It is built on what politicians have actually said in 2025–26, with every quote dated and linked to its source.

Key dates

  • Election day: Saturday 7 November 2026. Prime Minister Christopher Luxon announced the date on 21 January 2026. [RNZ]
  • Advance voting: Began on Monday 26 October 2026 (Labour Day), with party leaders among those voting on day one (RNZ, 26 Oct), and runs to Friday 6 November. Chief Electoral Officer Karl Le Quesne expects relatively small numbers in the first few days, picking up around the weekend and ramping up on the Thursday and Friday before election day (RNZ live blog, 26 Oct). First figures: Electoral Commission data showed 68,944 people voted on the first day (1.83% of enrolled voters), slightly more than the 65,845 on the first day in 2023; in 2020, when voting opened two days earlier, 105,489 voted on the same day (The Post; Newsroom, 27 Oct). The Commission will not track how many people are turned away because they are not enrolled (Newsroom). Running total: by the end of Thursday 29 October 455,522 people had voted (12.0% of enrolled voters), including 145,192 on the Thursday, compared with 339,032 (9.2%) at the same point in 2023 and 579,036 (16.3%) in 2020 (The Post's election dashboard, using daily Electoral Commission figures, checked 31 Oct; the Commission publishes each day's figure around 2pm the next day). The dashboard now puts the Wednesday 28 October total at 310,330 (309,876 in the Commission's first release, as reported by the NZ Herald, 29 Oct). On 29 October the Electoral Commission referred two people to police over claims they had voted 'multiple times'; police said the claims had not been verified (RNZ). Hipkins said he had heard anecdotally that more early votes than usual were special votes, and that the enrolment changes 'could actually slow down the vote count' (RNZ live blog, 29 Oct). We could not check the Commission's own site, which blocks our requests. Former Speaker Trevor Mallard (Labour) complained on X that the Wainuiomata advance voting place was closed on day one; Luxon called his comments 'intemperate' (same source). Voting is in person (online voting only for overseas voters). 207 voting places will open on Labour Day (Monday 26 October), offering 1,437 hours of voting, and more will open closer to election day; hours vary, so check vote.nz first. On election day, Saturday 7 November, more than 2,300 voting places will be open from 9am to 7pm. EasyVote cards are not required (Chief Electoral Officer Karl Le Quesne, 22 October). Electoral Commission advice released on 18 October called the date 'challenging, but manageable', partly because voting opens on a public holiday. [RNZ]
  • Enrolment: Closed at midnight on Sunday 25 October 2026 (RNZ, 26 October). You cannot enrol or update details after that, and you cannot enrol at a voting place. Chief Electoral Officer Karl Le Quesne said every complete application received by the deadline will be processed in time, including forms arriving in the post on Tuesday 27 October; enrolment events were held around the country on the final weekend (RNZ, 23 October). On 25 October Le Quesne told TVNZ's Q+A it was 'fair' to say the earlier deadline would affect the number enrolled, but 'hard to say' how much; the Commission will analyse the impact after the election (RNZ, 1News). Labour said some people had struck problems with the online enrolment form; the Commission said it was not aware of any technical issues (RNZ live blog, 25 Oct). [RNZ]
  • Enrolment figures: Latest (still provisional): on Friday 30 October the Electoral Commission put enrolment at 93.6% of eligible people (3,790,946), below 2023's 94.7% but above 2014 and 2017; 18–24-year-olds were on 74.2% (77% in 2023), up more than 20 points (about 85,000 people) since July. The provisional rate for people of Māori descent was 85.9%: 603,968 Māori are enrolled (567,012 in 2023), 331,455 (55%) on the Māori roll. Chief Electoral Officer Karl Le Quesne called it 'a reasonably healthy enrolment rate' and said the Commission would review its work and report to Parliament; to match 2023 another 40,000 people would have needed to enrol. RNZ says the final enrolment figures won't be known until after the election (RNZ, 30 Oct). The Post's dashboard, from Commission data, gives 93.57% and an estimated 260,694 eligible people not enrolled (The Post); Hipkins said about 'a quarter of a million' eligible people could not vote and blamed the end of same-day enrolment (RNZ live blog, 30 Oct). Also on 30 October the Commission said up to 6,800 people with residence class visas had been wrongly asked for immigration information when applying to enrol; it is contacting them, and anyone eligible who applied before the 25 October deadline can still cast a special vote (freephone 0800 36 76 56). Labour's Camilla Belich called it an extra barrier (RNZ, 30 Oct). Earlier: as of Wednesday 28 October the Commission said 93.5% were enrolled (3,787,116), including 73.8% of 18–24-year-olds (NZ Herald, 29 Oct; the Commission's own site blocks our checks). Earlier (provisional): on 27 October the Electoral Commission's figures put enrolment at 92.8% of eligible people and 71.2% of 18–24-year-olds, with forms still being processed and the final count due this week; Chief Electoral Officer Karl Le Quesne said matching 2023's 94.7% would be 'a bit of a stretch' (RNZ live blog, 27 Oct; The Post, which gives 3,759,992 enrolled). Newsroom reported provisional data putting the unenrolled at nearly 300,000. Luxon told Morning Report on 27 October he expected enrolment to end up around or above 2014 (92.6%) and 2017 (92.4%) levels; Labour's Tangi Utikere called the coalition's changes 'anti-democratic' (RNZ). Earlier: on 26 October the Electoral Commission said about 92.5% of eligible voters were enrolled after a weekend flood of enrolments, with the final figure still being counted and expected on Thursday 29 October (RNZ live blog, 26 Oct; RNZ on 27 Oct described the 92.5% as the position as of Friday). Before that: as of Friday 23 October, with two days left, 92.4% of eligible people had enrolled, compared with 94.7% in 2023 (RNZ, 26 Oct), and about 70% of 18–24-year-olds, compared with about 77% on election day 2023 (1News, 25 Oct). These broadly match the unverified figures below. Earlier: as at 21 October, 3,729,667 people were enrolled, 92.1% of eligible voters, below the 94.7% reached in 2023 but already more than the 3,688,292 enrolled at that election. 18–24-year-olds remain the least enrolled (302,475, or 68.6%). These are Electoral Commission figures released on 22 October (RNZ). (An earlier RNZ report on 22 October gave 91.2% and 65.5%; the Commission's newer release supersedes it.) A later Commission release, reported only by a secondary source we could read (The Gist, 24 Oct; the Commission's own site blocks our checks), gave 92.2% enrolled and 307,991 eligible people still unenrolled as at 23 October, with 18–24-year-olds on 69.9% (77.2% in 2023); treat these as unverified. Enrol online at vote.nz, by post or at an enrolment hub. Same-day enrolment during advance voting and on election day has been abolished. [RNZ]
  • Overseas voting: Opened on Wednesday 21 October 2026, along with telephone dictation voting for blind and some disabled voters. About 126,000 enrolled voters have told the Electoral Commission they live overseas (92,600 in 2023), 64% of them in Australia (Electoral Commission figures, RNZ live blog, 21 October). By 25 October Le Quesne said more than 132,000 New Zealanders overseas had enrolled, about two-thirds of them in Australia, and just over 20,000 had downloaded voting papers; the Commission sent 2.2 million email reminders, followed by texts (RNZ, 25 Oct). Some overseas voters reported that Adobe Reader's 'Fill & Sign' tool would not let them tick some parties' circles; the Commission said the voting paper is a standard PDF, not a fillable form, that voters can annotate manually, use another reader or print and fill it in by hand, and that a vote counts as long as the voter's intention is clear (1News, 24 Oct). [RNZ]
  • Recent debates: Coming up: the Q+A People's Debate, with audience questions to figures from National, Labour, the Greens, ACT, NZ First, Te Pāti Māori and Opportunity, is live on TVNZ1, TVNZ+ and RNZ at 7pm on Thursday 5 November (1News, 28 Oct). TVNZ's Kaupapa Māori Debate (Thursday 29 October, hosted by Scotty Morrison and Te Aniwa Hurihanganui) brought together Willie Jackson (Labour), Tama Potaka (National), Nicole McKee (ACT), Marama Davidson (Greens), Shane Jones (NZ First), Eden Skipper (Opportunity) and Rawiri Waititi (Te Pāti Māori), clashing over Te Tiriti, gangs, grocery prices, jobs, marae funding and te reo (RNZ; full video, 1News). Whakaata Māori's Whakatau 2026 Māori leaders' debate, moderated by Moana Maniapoto, was held on Wednesday 28 October with Tama Potaka (National), Marama Davidson (Greens), Willie Jackson (Labour), Debbie Ngarewa-Packer (Te Pāti Māori) and Shane Jones (NZ First) (Te Ao Māori News). Local: a Waitaki candidates' forum in Wānaka on 27 October included ACT leader David Seymour (RNZ). Earlier: the third and final Luxon v Hipkins head-to-head (Stuff and The Post) was held on Tuesday 20 October. The NZ Herald 'Powerbrokers' debate, moderated by Ryan Bridge, was held on Wednesday 21 October with Marama Davidson (Greens), David Seymour (ACT), Winston Peters (NZ First) and Qiulae Wong (Opportunity). Te Pāti Māori did not take part after the death of co-leader Debbie Ngarewa-Packer's mother (RNZ live blog, 21 October). [RNZ]
  • Official results: The official count is due on Friday 27 November 2026. Luxon said formal coalition talks would probably wait for it, and neither he nor Hipkins would promise a deal before Christmas (RNZ, 19 October). On 26 October Hipkins changed tack and committed to forming a Labour-led government before Christmas (RNZ); Luxon said 'it'll take as long as it takes' (RNZ live blog). [RNZ]
  • Parliament: The 54th Parliament was dissolved on 1 October 2026. Ministers stay in office until a new government is formed. [Wikipedia]
  • Four-year term referendum: This will not be held with the 2026 election. The government dropped the planned referendum on 18 February 2026 (secondary source: Wikipedia). [Wikipedia]

The story so far

The current government is the Sixth National Government, formed in November 2023. National leads it with two separate coalition agreements, one with ACT and one with NZ First. Winston Peters was Deputy Prime Minister until 31 May 2025, when David Seymour took over under the agreed rotation.

Seats at dissolution: National 49, ACT 11 and NZ First 8 (government total 68). Labour 34, Greens 15, Te Pāti Māori 4 and two independents (both former Te Pāti Māori MPs). That is 123 seats in total.

Polls in the final month show both major parties well below their 2023 results (64.9% together), and the two latest polls, taken over almost the same days, disagree on who leads. The RNZ–Reid Research poll (20–26 October, 1000 eligible voters online, ±3.1%, published 29 October) had Labour on 30.6% (down 0.2) and National on 24.8% (down 1.1), below its 2020 result of 25.6%; Greens 14.5%, ACT 10.1% (up 1.1, ahead of NZ First), NZ First 9.9%, Opportunity 7.2% (up 1.7) and Te Pāti Māori 1.9%. On RNZ's projection Labour (37 seats), the Greens (18) and Opportunity (9) would have 64 of 120 seats and the coalition 54, assuming Te Pāti Māori wins one or two electorates. Preferred PM: Hipkins 23.3% (up 1.4), Luxon 17.1%; 53.8% said Luxon was doing poorly as PM and 30.1% well, while Hipkins' rating turned positive (39.7% well, 34.7% poorly). 50.5% said the country was heading in the wrong direction (RNZ, 29 Oct). National campaign chair Simeon Brown said the poll was 'out of step with most of the other polls that we're seeing, including our own'; Seymour called some movements an 'outlier'; Hipkins said it showed 'real momentum' for change. The 1News–Verian poll (21–25 October, 1000 eligible voters, ±3.1%, published 27 October) had National down three points to 29% (28.9%) and Labour unchanged on 27% (27.3%), Labour's fifth poll in a row in the upper 20s; Greens 15% (14.8%), ACT 12% (12.4%, a term high), NZ First 10%, Opportunity 6% (6.3%) and Te Pāti Māori 0.1%, its lowest in the series (it is running a candidate-vote-only campaign). On 1News' projections National, ACT and NZ First would have 61 seats: enough to govern if Te Pāti Māori wins only one electorate (a 121-seat Parliament), but two short of the 63 needed if it holds four seats and Mariameno Kapa-Kingi keeps Te Tai Tokerau (125 seats), in which case Opportunity's eight MPs would decide. The left bloc (Labour, Greens, Te Pāti Māori, Kapa-Kingi) would have 56 seats in that scenario and 64 with Opportunity. Preferred PM: Luxon 24% (up four, his best of the campaign), Hipkins 18%. 11% were undecided or refused on the party vote (1News, 27 Oct; RNZ). Luxon said 'the only poll that matters is November 7th'; Hipkins said it would come down to turnout; Peters said 'we don't believe in these polls'. A week earlier (15–19 October) the same poll had National 32% and Labour 27%, while The Post/Freshwater Strategy poll (9–19 October, published 20 October) had Labour narrowly ahead (28.7% v 27.6%) with Opportunity on 5.4%. Earlier, the 13 October Verian poll had the majors tied on 28%, and a leaked 6–10 October Anacta poll (Labour's own pollster) had Labour 29%, National 28%.

Leader ratings: in 1News Vote Compass (136,713 participants, 4–11 October, weighted, non-probability), no leader averaged 5 out of 10 for favourability. Hipkins was highest on 4.3, then Swarbrick 4.0, Wong 3.8, Peters 3.7 and Luxon 3.6; Seymour 3.4, Davidson 3.3, Ngarewa-Packer 2.1 and Waititi 1.9 (1News, 17 Oct).

Health: in the 20 October 1News–Verian poll 70% of voters said the public health system is in crisis and 28% said it faces challenges but is not in crisis (1News, 20 Oct). Health has become the second-biggest campaign issue after the cost of living, and dominated the final leaders' debate.

Cost of living and schools: in the same 15–19 October 1News–Verian poll (published 21 October), National and Labour were tied on 28% as the party voters most trust to tackle the cost of living, ahead of the Greens (10%), ACT (7%), NZ First (6%), Opportunity (3%) and Te Pāti Māori (1%); 17% named none or did not know. 70% wanted to keep the rule that primary schools teach an hour each of reading, writing and maths every day, and 24% wanted it removed (1News, 21 Oct).

Inflation: annual inflation eased to 3.7% in the September quarter, from 4.1% (Stats NZ, 22 October), and is expected to rise again once the fuel-price spike is captured. Nicola Willis called it encouraging; Labour's Willow-Jean Prime pointed to unemployment at an 11-year high and 30,000 more people on Jobseeker; David Seymour said that without fuel prices inflation was within the Reserve Bank's band (RNZ; RNZ live blog, 22 Oct). On 28 October the Reserve Bank unexpectedly held the official cash rate at 2.75% in a split decision, when a rise had been expected (1News). Willis said a Labour-led 'four-headed' coalition would put lower rates at risk; Labour's Barbara Edmonds said Willis was 'celebrating a sluggish economy'; Swarbrick said 'higher mortgage prices cannot reopen the Strait of Hormuz' (RNZ live blog, 28 Oct).

Late deciders: in the final Post/Freshwater Strategy poll (9–19 October, 1458 voters, ±3%), only 14% said they always vote for the same party; 34% had decided in the past few months (12% in the past few weeks) and 15% were still undecided. Half planned to vote on election day and 34% during advance voting. 26% of NZ First supporters and 23% of Opportunity supporters had decided in the past few weeks. In Auckland (426 likely voters) National led Labour 37.7% to 26.4%, while Hipkins was preferred PM over Luxon 45.9% to 41.1% (The Post, 23 Oct; The Post, 22 Oct).

Tax on the wealthy: in 1News Vote Compass (186,475 participants, 4–21 October, weighted, non-probability), 62% said wealthier people should pay somewhat or much more tax, down from 67% in 2023 but a majority in every Vote Compass survey since 2014, including 53% of those earning over $100,000. By current party support: Greens 96%, Labour 88%, Opportunity 86%, NZ First 45%, National 30%, ACT 25% (1News, 24 Oct).

Where voters differ from their party: 1News Vote Compass (185,371 participants, 4–27 October, weighted, non-probability) found the generational tobacco-sales ban was the biggest gap for all three coalition parties: each strongly opposes it, but their supporters lean towards supporting it (National supporters averaged 3.66 on a 1–5 scale). The biggest gap overall was Te Pāti Māori's opposition to a cap on council rates (its supporters averaged 4.12); for Labour it was transgender women in sport, and for Opportunity limits on rent increases (1News, 29 Oct).

Big issues of 2026: the cost of living (made worse by a fuel-price spike linked to the 2026 Middle East conflict); tax (Labour's capital gains tax, the Greens' wealth taxes, National's 'no new taxes' pledge); two challenges to Luxon's leadership inside National (April and August); the law replacing the RMA, passed in September; Treaty and Waitangi Tribunal policy; immigration; and turmoil inside Te Pāti Māori.

How this was made

For each party in Parliament we profile the leader(s) plus four other senior figures, mainly the next places on the party's 2026 list (details on each party page). Quotes were collected only from sources we actually retrieved: news reports (RNZ, 1News, Newsroom, The Spinoff, Te Ao Māori News and others), party and ministerial press releases, and politicians' own X posts. If a quote could not be verified, it was left out. Where a person has fewer than four verified quotes, we say so. Full methodology and source list →
